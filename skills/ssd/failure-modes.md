# SSD architectural failure modes

Use these patterns to challenge an analysis. The corrective action is to return to the semantic model, not to add more SSD terminology.

| Failure mode | Diagnostic signal | Why it violates the source | Required correction | Eval evidence of failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Screen-first modeling | The answer begins with pages, tables, forms, navigation, or component layout. | Surfaces are contextual projections; entities, relationships, capabilities, and constraints are primary. | Remove the surface temporarily and reconstruct the minimum semantic model. | Core entities or rules cannot be explained without referencing UI. |
| Affordance/capability confusion | A “Delete button,” menu item, endpoint, or tool is named as the capability. | Controls invoke declared possibilities to cause change; they do not define them. | Specify target, inputs, preconditions, authority, effects, postconditions, and responsibility independently. | Swapping UI for a tool silently changes the contract or leaves it undefined. |
| Projection authority | Hidden/disabled UI or an unavailable tool is the permission system. | Ability and authority differ; authority is a semantic rule. | Define enforceable action/entity/context/scope authority and project its explanation on every surface. | Direct invocation bypasses the supposed permission. |
| Semantic decoration | Existing screen-first architecture is retained and entities, JSON-LD, or metadata are added around it. | SSD is recentering on meaning, not adding metadata. | Show how explicit meaning changes capability boundaries, state, authority, consequence, or projection decisions. | Removing metadata leaves all architectural decisions unchanged. |
| Human/agent divergence | Agent tools map mechanically to buttons or permit different rules/outcomes. | Human and machine interfaces are equal projections of one meaning with strict parity. | Compare contracts capability by capability and reconcile identity, rules, authority, effects, and evidence. | One channel can create a state or consequence the other cannot explain. |
| Hidden side effects | Only the requested entity or primary outcome is modeled. | Side effects can affect entities, permissions, automations, and assumptions. | Sweep connected entities and downstream effects; add observation, recovery, and audit. | Evaluation prompt names an affected party or automation that the response ignores. |
| Irreversibility theater | Safety consists of “Are you sure?” or a generic approval step. | Irreversibility requires explicit naming, gating, uncertainty blocking, and permanent audit. | Define the irreversible boundary, gate conditions, recovery limits, responsibility, and evidence. | The same action could execute through an agent tool without equivalent validation. |
| Authority collapse | Possession of a role, endpoint, tool, or technical ability is assumed to authorize action. | Authority states who may act, on which entities, under which conditions and scope. | Apply least privilege, expiry, escalation, and explicit privilege-use audit. | Role labels replace contextual authority or agent access is unbounded. |
| State/variant collapse | Color, component variant, step number, or route encodes lifecycle truth. | States alter allowed behavior; variants alter presentation only. | Name the state machine and derive every variant or route from it. | Behavior changes when styling/configuration changes without a semantic transition. |
| Flow fundamentalism | A requested wizard or process sequence is accepted as product architecture. | Flow should emerge from state, decision edges, intent, constraints, authority, and uncertainty. | Model states and legal transitions, then choose a contextual projection. | Alternative entry points, partial completion, or agent operation cannot be represented. |
| Context-free operator | The answer says “the user” despite materially different actors and power. | Operators differ in intent, authority, risk tolerance, and accountability. | Separate operators and record their intent, authority, constraints, and responsibility. | Administrator, subject, reviewer, service, and agent share unexplained permissions. |
| Confirmation as intent | A click or confirmation is treated as sufficient intent evidence. | Intent includes outcome, reason, constraints, and acceptable risk. | Capture structured intent proportional to consequence and preserve it in the record. | Audit can show a click but cannot explain the desired outcome or acceptable risk. |
| Logs-as-audit | Raw logs or a generic dashboard are offered as accountability. | Auditability is navigable explanation linked to actors, rules, entities, and time. | Design risk-proportional human and machine audit projections with immutable history. | An investigator cannot answer who, why allowed, affected entity, or response path. |
| Unsafe retry | Failure handling retries until success or treats halt as a crash. | Guardrails require deterministic halt/escalation; safe stops preserve context and prevent irreversible action. | Name halt conditions, preserved context, authority transfer, and recovery path. | Retry repeats beyond a guardrail or duplicates side effects. |
| Premature completion | Unknown semantics are filled by plausible domain assumptions. | SSD requires explicit boundaries, uncertainty, and “I don't know/out of scope/human review” exits. | Classify assumptions, raise open semantic questions, and stop consequential work when blocking. | Confident prose contains unsourced thresholds, authority, or recovery promises. |
| Vocabulary imitation | SSD nouns appear throughout, but architecture remains screen-, CRUD-, or workflow-first. | Competence is a change in design decisions, not recall. | Apply the projection-removal and vocabulary-removal tests; compare against a no-skill control. | Same architecture survives unchanged when SSD terms are removed. |
